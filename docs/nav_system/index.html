🐢 How a Hope Turtle Finds Its Way

The turtleOS navigation system — what our solar sailboats can do today, what we still have to prove, and the three-stream roadmap (turtle · shore · bale) to full learning autonomy. Written for the whole team: no engineering background required.

July 2026 · Aboard · Ashore · Bale mesh · Only "Today" items are built

The Big IdeaA turtle senses, thinks, and trims — forever

Every Hope Turtle is a small autonomous sailboat. It has no motor for propulsion — the wind pushes it, and a tiny computer (the XIAO ESP32-S3, about the size of a postage stamp) decides everything. The turtle has exactly one moving part: the sail, rotated by a servo motor. The rudder is fixed. About three times every second, the computer runs one loop of the same routine:

How a sail-only boat actually steers — the honest version

This is the fact everything else follows from. The turtle has no movable rudder. Trimming the sail does not point the boat the way a steering wheel points a car. It changes the force the wind puts on the boat; the fixed rudder, hull, and keel then convert that force into some mix of forward motion and turning:

Why this matters: it sets the order of all the work that follows. First measure whether a 10° sail change produces a repeatable heading response, and only then automate. An automatic controller wrapped around an unknown relationship is an elegant way to automate confusion — so every rung of the ladder in Part Three earns the next one with evidence.

Part OneWhat the turtle can do today Running now

The turtle's life story: five states

From the moment it powers on, the turtle is always in exactly one of five "states" — think of them as chapters of its voyage. The state is reported home to hopeturtles.org with every telemetry message, so we always know which chapter a turtle is in.

Why SAFE matters

A confused robot boat that keeps sailing is dangerous to itself. SAFE means: stop trying, "feather" the sail so the wind slides past, keep reporting position home. A human (or a recovery rule) decides what happens next.

What ARRIVAL honestly means

A sail-only boat with a fixed rudder can't park. ARRIVAL means: inside the target circle → feather the sail, report "arrived", and keep sending position while drifting. If drift carries it back out, it sails in again. No station-keeping is claimed.

The clever trick: finding the wind with no wind sensor

Sailboats need to know the wind direction, but wind vanes are fragile and our turtles don't carry one. Instead, the turtle does something a human sailor would recognize: it sweeps the sail slowly from one side to the other and feels for the flutter. When the sail points straight into the wind it stops "filling" and starts flapping — sailors call this luffing. A little angle sensor on the sail (the AS5600) feels that flapping as jitter.

The turtle re-runs this sweep every 10 minutes while sailing. The plan makes re-sweeps event-driven — sweep when confidence in the wind estimate drops, or after a big turn — with the fixed timer relaxing to a 30–60 minute backup. Later, once turtles can talk to each other (Part Five), a neighbour's fresh wind solution can save a sweep entirely.

What else is already working

🧭 Compass heading

A magnetometer (the GY-271 board) tells the turtle which way it's pointing, corrected for local magnetic declination.

📍 Waypoint bearing

The turtle carries a list of GPS waypoints, computes the great-circle bearing to the next one, and advances waypoints on arrival — progress is saved so a reboot doesn't lose it.

🎛️ Placeholder autopilot

A simple heading-error → sail-nudge formula exists in the code — but it's scaffolding, not a tuned controller. Until we measure how sail moves actually turn the boat (Part Three), no controller can honestly be tuned.

🛟 Basic safety net

GPS silent too long → SAFE. Battery low → feather the sail to save power. Every telemetry message reports the machine state to hopeturtles.org.

Quiet superpowers we already have. Three foundations make the experiments in Part Three possible without new infrastructure: GPS logging gives ground truth — position, course over ground, speed over ground, and progress toward or away from the waypoint; the AS5600 sail sensor reports where the sail actually is, not just where we told the servo to go; and the telemetry schema already carries values_json, confidence_json, and flags_json with GPS coordinates and separate device/server timestamps — every experiment logs straight through it.

Part TwoThe new hardware: our IMU Sourced & wired

The big July decision: every turtle gets an IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit) — a fingernail-sized motion sensor, the same kind of chip that lets your phone know when you rotate it. It gives the turtle heading, heel (tilt), turn rate, and a feel for wave disturbance — the senses everything in Part Three builds on.

Phase 0 — do this before anything else. The IMU is sourced, wired, and already sitting at a free address (0x69) — the only thing left is code, so it jumps the queue ahead of AOELL, ahead of the sailing experiments, ahead of everything. Four concrete steps:

  1. Confirm the chip on the bench (it answers 0x71 to a "who are you?" register read) and write the MPU-9250 driver — its register map is different from the plainer MPU6050 some listings confuse it with.
  2. Pick it up automatically at boot: scan for it alongside the other sensors, log [BOOT] MPU-9250 found, and confirm whether it needs any settle time before its first reading can be trusted (to be measured, not assumed).
  3. Ditch the old parts, don't run two in parallel. The old GY-271 compass board and the old 6-degree-of-freedom sensing path get retired in favor of this one integrated 9-measurement chip — gyroscope, accelerometer, and compass together.
  4. Wire the new driver straight into the screens that already exist — the compass and sailpoint screens — so the swap is visible and testable on the bench immediately, before any of the fancier fusion math is written.

What the IMU senses

🌀 Gyroscope

"How fast am I turning?" Silky-smooth from second to second, but slowly drifts over minutes — like a dancer counting spins with closed eyes.

📉 Accelerometer

"Which way is down?" Tells us how much the boat is heeled (tilted) — essential for correcting the compass on a leaning sailboat — and how roughly the waves are shaking it.

🧲 Magnetometer

"Which way is north?" Truthful on average but noisy moment-to-moment — and wrong when tilted, unless we correct it with the accelerometer.

🤝 Together: sensor fusion

Blend the gyro's smoothness with the compass's honesty and the accelerometer's sense of "down", and you get a heading that is smooth, true, and tilt-proof.

Wiring — already resolved

All our sensors share a single two-wire party line called the I2C bus, and each device on it needs a unique address, like houses on a street. The IMU's factory address (0x68) collided with the clock chip, which already lived there — so the module is strapped, on its own board, from its address-select pin (AD0) to its own 3.3 V supply. That moves it to 0x69, which is free. This is done and wired; nothing here is an open question anymore, and Phase 0 above is where the remaining work — the code side — actually lives.

Bonus: the MPU-9250's built-in compass appears on the bus at its own address (0x0C), which doesn't clash with anything — including the old GY-271 compass at 0x0D. That lets us run both side by side on the bench for one last comparison before the GY-271 is retired for good, per Phase 0.

Part ThreeThe genuine upgrade: a heading you can trust For review

With the IMU aboard, the single concrete win over today's system is fusing three senses into one honest heading. The rest of the plan — the experimental ladder, the cadence rules, tacking, the safety net — is covered in full in Part Four (the AOELL learning loop) and Part Six (the phase-by-phase ladder), so it isn't repeated here.

A heading you can trust: sensor fusion

Today's compass-only heading is like a nervous witness — right on average, jittery in the moment, and unreliable when the boat heels. The fusion recipe (a "complementary filter") blends 98% gyro smoothness with 2% compass truth on every sample, while the accelerometer un-tilts the compass math:

The IMU also gives us spin detection: the gyro notices the boat starting to rotate long before the compass average does. Important honesty note — detection is all it gives us for now. We do not yet know whether any sail movement reliably arrests a spin, or accidentally worsens one. So spin events are flagged and reported home, and the "what should the sail do about it?" question is answered by experiment, not assumption.

Part FourLearning twice: AOELL and hopeturtles.org For review

This is what the whole system is for. turtleOS is not a finished navigation controller — it is an instrumented sailing experiment that acts, observes the consequences, evaluates honestly, records enough context to explain itself, and learns. And it must learn twice: once aboard for the next decision, and again ashore — on hopeturtles.org — so the whole team, and eventually the whole fleet, improves.

The AOELL cycle: every action is a little experiment

We call the onboard loop AOELL: Act · Observe · Evaluate · Log · Learn. Even "do nothing" is a recorded action — hold periods are the control cases that let us tell a sail effect apart from a current or a wind shift that would have happened anyway. But the cycle isn't complete when the turtle updates itself. The records sail on to shore, where humans and (later) models interpret them and send back improved, versioned instructions:

📖 The log records reasoning

Every cycle answers seven questions: what did I do, what did I believe, why did I choose it, what did I predict, what did I observe, how did I score it, what did I update — plus which firmware, policy, and config were in force.

🤷 "Not evaluable" is not failure

A lost GPS fix or a wave-thrashed observation window must never become a false training label. The turtle is allowed — required — to say "I couldn't judge that one."

📦 Nothing is lost at sea

Connectivity is intermittent, so records queue aboard and upload in checksummed, acknowledged batches. A week-late upload appears in the week-old part of the voyage — never masquerading as live navigation.

🔒 No self-rewriting at sea

Onboard learning updates bounded state inside a versioned policy — logged and reversible. New policies arrive signed and checksummed, and the turtle keeps a one-step rollback.

Three development streams that must rejoin

This gives our work three parallel streams. Stream A is the turtleOS firmware — the sailing ladder from Part Three plus the AOELL recording machinery. Stream B is hopeturtles.org — the mission database, the batch ingestion, and above all the mission_review panel, the major later-phase destination everything aboard is working toward connecting to. Stream C is the bale mesh of Part Five — the turtle-to-turtle radio network that decides how much of the evidence ever reaches either of the other two. No stream is useful alone: perfect logs nobody can interpret teach nothing, a beautiful dashboard with no causal records explains nothing, and both are limited by what survives the sea.

Inside mission_review: the shore-side half

The mission_review panel on hopeturtles.org is not a dashboard — it is a scientific notebook and review system. Its map draws four different things and refuses to blur them: where the turtle actually went, where the mission intended it to go, where turtleOS predicted an action would take it, and what was evaluated afterward. Keeping those distinct is how we tell three very different failures apart — wrong objective, wrong prediction, or a good decision defeated by the sea.

🗺️ Mission map + time scrubber

Actual / intended / predicted / evaluated layers, action markers, prediction error vectors, confidence shading. Drag the scrubber and every panel follows to the same moment and cycle.

📈 Synchronized timeline

Sail commanded vs. measured, wind estimate + confidence, heading vs. course over ground, VMG, battery, state changes — all on one aligned time axis, so causality can be seen instead of guessed.

🔍 Cycle inspector

Click any action: the turtle's belief before, the trigger, alternatives it rejected, its prediction, what actually happened at each horizon, and what it learned. Reviewers can confirm or question labels — originals are never erased.

📊 Sense-making views

Aggregations across many cycles: response by sail step size and wind angle, hold vs. intervention periods, energy per correction, prediction calibration ("70% confident" should be right ~70% of the time), per-turtle differences.

Machine learning grows in careful stages — response tables first (is there a learnable relationship at all?), then supervised models validated on held-out missions, then shadow mode where a candidate only says what it would do while the trusted policy keeps the sail. Only a model that beats simple baselines, stays calibrated, and knows when to say "I don't know" ever gets deployed — signed, versioned, monitored, and one step from rollback. No turtle silently rewrites its own brain at sea.

The governing principle: act deliberately, observe carefully, evaluate honestly, log completely — and learn twice: once aboard for the next decision, and again ashore so the whole team, and eventually the whole turtle fleet, can improve.

Part FiveThe bale: turtles that talk to each other Planning only

Everything in Part Four assumes the evidence gets home. Today it only does if the hull gets home. A turtle records to its own memory and waits for a shore connection that, on a real mission, most likely never comes until it arrives — and a turtle lost at sea takes every reading it ever took down with it. That is a learning system with a single point of failure per turtle.

But turtles are cheap: open source, built from wood and locally available materials, carrying perhaps forty dollars of electronics. They will be deployed in numbers, and they will drift in loose company. There are flocks of sheep and swarms of wasps — and there are bales of turtles. Real sea turtles coordinate migrations and mass arrivals through the ocean. If ours can talk to each other over long-range radio, the unit of survival stops being the turtle and becomes the fleet.

What a bale gives us

📦 Data survives the hull

Records replicate to neighbours, so losing a turtle stops being a data loss. A reading taken on day 2 should have several independent copies by day 5.

🍃 Shared wind, saved battery

A luff sweep costs battery and interrupts steering. A neighbour ten kilometres away is probably in the same wind field — so its fresh solution can seed, or entirely skip, your next sweep.

🚩 Shared hazards

A turtle becalmed or pushed backwards for six hours has learned something about that patch of ocean. Broadcasting "this area cost me a day" lets the bale route around it — an empirical current map the fleet builds itself.

📡 Anyone can be the gateway

Any turtle that passes a harbour, a vessel, or a shore access point flushes not just its own queue but everything it is carrying for the others.

🐢 The mother turtle

Same hull, extra storage, bigger battery, no sailing mission — deployed to sit among the bale and listen. Recovering one mother turtle recovers the whole bale's log. Far cheaper than recovering every hull.

🔬 A real experiment, replicated

Many hulls, same water, same weather, different policies and rigs, sharing a clock and positions. That is a far stronger scientific design than a scatter of isolated turtles, however well instrumented each one is.

Why the sea is unusually good for this

Mesh radio networks have a reputation for being fussy and disappointing. That reputation was earned on land, and almost every reason for it is absent at sea:

👀 Nothing in the way

Long-range radio is defeated by buildings, hills, and trees. Open water has none of them — the limit becomes the horizon rather than the signal.

🤫 A quiet spectrum

The radio bands we'd use are crowded in any city and nearly silent mid-ocean. The same radio is a different instrument out there.

🌊 Drift correlates

Turtles released together are pushed by the same current and wind. They spread out slowly and coherently, so neighbours stay neighbours for days — the network's shape changes by the hour, not by the second.

🐌 Nobody is in a hurry

A voyage lasts weeks. A record is just as useful three days late as three seconds late. That removes the hardest constraint in mesh design: we need eventual delivery, which is a far cheaper problem.

🔗 The relays are the mission

We can't afford to deploy a relay network — and we don't have to. Every turtle launched to sail somewhere is simultaneously a node. The network gets denser for free as the project succeeds.

➕ Strictly additive

A turtle with no radio, or one that hears nobody, behaves exactly as turtles behave today: queue to memory and sail on. There is no flag day, and a mixed fleet is the expected setup for the first deployments.

The honest counterweight: antenna height. A turtle's antenna sits perhaps 30 cm above the water, and in any real swell it spends part of its life below the wave crests. Links will appear and vanish within seconds even when the average shape of the bale is stable for days — something no flat-water test will reveal. That is exactly why the design is store-and-forward: a turtle hands over what it can, whenever it can, and never depends on holding a conversation.

What has to be decided before anything is built

Nothing here is committed to — no hardware chosen, no code written. This part of the plan exists so the decisions that are expensive to reverse get made deliberately rather than discovered halfway through:

📐 The message format

A full telemetry message is 200–400 bytes of text; a long-range radio packet holds about 51. A packed 20-byte record (who, when, where, state, battery, wind) fits comfortably — but this format is the single most expensive thing in the project to change later, because turtles, relays, and the shore server must agree on it forever. It gets written down first, with a version number built in.

🔌 A second small brain

The XIAO's pins are fully spoken for — two free, seven needed for a radio. The recommended answer is a tiny second chip that owns the radio and talks to the turtle over the wires the sensors already share, so a radio problem can never stall the sailing loop.

⚖️ Airtime is rationed by law

Radio regulations cap how much any node may transmit, and that budget is shared between a turtle's own messages and the ones it relays for others. Which region's rules apply — and what applies in international waters — needs a real answer, not an assumption. It sets the whole message budget.

🔋 Power, measured not guessed

Transmitting is expensive, and a busy relay can spend real energy carrying other turtles' traffic. Before any design work, we measure actual draw with the battery monitor already aboard.

Beyond those, four questions are for the team rather than the code: which radio region and band; whether the first bale is 3 hulls or 30 (below about 5 there is rarely a relay path, and the value is mostly data survival); whether a mother turtle is in the first deployment or a later one; and what added cost per hull is acceptable — if the radio doubles the price of a deliberately cheap turtle, launching twice as many isolated turtles may be the better experiment.

The full bale plan — the vision, the pin arithmetic, the packed record layout, the mesh rules (deduplication, hop limits, provenance, what happens when two halves of a bale meet again after a week apart), and the open questions — is in docs/bale_network_vision.md, with the phased task list as Stream C in docs/navigation_roadmap.md.

Part SixThe experimental ladder — phases for review

We propose climbing in this order, with a measurable pass/fail gate at each rung. Rungs 2–5 are Stream A's sailing experiments; the Stream B build (mission_review) runs in parallel and meets them at the J milestones. Each phase earns the next. None of this starts until the team has reviewed this proposal.

  1. AOELL foundations. The cycle record (act, belief, reason, prediction, observation, evaluation, versions), the local queue, and resumable checksummed upload — so every experiment after this one explains itself. Gate: one cycle reconstructable from logs; delayed and duplicate uploads produce one ordered server record.
  2. Sensor fusion. Verify the IMU's identity, write the MPU-9250 driver, fuse gyro + compass + tilt into one trustworthy heading; spin detection as a reported event only. Gate: heading drift under 2°/min on the bench, correct at 30° of heel.
  3. Luff validation. Measure the wind sweep's real accuracy against a known reference, on water; attach a confidence score to every solve; log every sweep as an AOELL cycle. Gate: a measured accuracy figure — no more assumed numbers.
  4. Propulsion. Demonstrate that chosen sail trims produce real forward progress, judged by the GPS track, with hold periods as controls. Gate: repeatable progress-made-good at trial trims.
  5. Turning influence. Bounded ±5°/±10°/±20° sail steps against hold baselines; measure the course response at 30 s / 2 min / 5 min; quantify repeatability — including whether anything arrests a spin. Gate: a usable sail→turn model, or an honest "no" that reshapes the plan.
  6. Heading control. Only now, wrap a controller (PID or simpler) around the demonstrated model, with deadband and slew limits. Gate: holds a heading within ±10° in calm water.
  7. Tacking. The upwind zig-zag, plus a re-sweep after every turn. Gate: net upwind progress across a full tack sequence.
  8. Safety net. Dead reckoning, GPS sanity check, geofence, hull sensors, honest ARRIVAL. Gate: goes SAFE within 60 s of GPS loss; sail feathers within 1 s.
  9. Unattended endurance. Watchdog + automatic mission resume. Gate: a mid-mission reboot resumes the voyage with no human touch.

Meanwhile Stream B climbs its own ladder on hopeturtles.org — mission + AOELL database tables, idempotent batch ingestion, the mission_review replay (map, timeline, cycle inspector), annotation and comparison views, then staged models and the signed policy lifecycle. Stream C climbs a third: the message format on paper, then a transport layer, a turtle-to-turtle link, the store-and-forward mesh, the bale view ashore, and finally shared wind and hazard knowledge.

The three ladders meet at the J milestones: J1 first replayed voyage (after rung 1), J2 trustworthy training data (after the experiment rungs), J3 shadow mode at sea, J4 the closed learning loop — the roadmap's definition of done for the learning architecture — and J5, a record taken by one turtle and carried home by another: the definition of done for evidence survival.

For the engineering-level task list behind these phases — file by file, checkbox by checkbox, all three streams and the J milestones — see docs/navigation_roadmap.md in the repository. The full AOELL specification (event contract, database tables, API surface, ML stages, and statistical safeguards) is in docs/AOELL nav revision.md, and the bale mesh plan is in docs/bale_network_vision.md. AppendixLittle glossary

I2C bus
A two-wire "party line" that lets many sensors share the same pair of wires, each answering to its own address.
IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit)
A chip that senses rotation (gyroscope), tilt and motion (accelerometer), and — on our MPU-9250 — magnetic north (magnetometer).
AOELL
The onboard cycle — Act, Observe, Evaluate, Log, Learn. Every action, even "hold the sail," becomes a recorded experiment with a prediction and an honestly scored outcome.
Bale
The collective noun for turtles — and our name for a group of turtles deployed together that share records and lessons by radio, so no single hull is the only copy of its own voyage.
Store-and-forward
Passing a message on whenever a neighbour happens to be in range, rather than holding a live connection. The right design when links come and go with the swell and nothing is urgent.
Mother turtle
A turtle with no sailing mission, extra storage and a bigger battery, deployed to sit among a bale and record everything it hears — the fleet's black box.
Gateway
Whichever turtle currently has a connection to shore. It uplinks not only its own readings but everything it is carrying for the rest of the bale.
mission_review
The planned analysis panel on hopeturtles.org: a synchronized map, timeline, and cycle inspector for replaying any voyage and understanding what the turtle believed, did, predicted, and observed.
Hold period
A stretch where the sail deliberately isn't moved. The control case — without holds, we can't tell a sail effect from a current or wind shift that would have happened anyway.
Shadow mode
A candidate model rides along recording what it would have done while the trusted policy keeps control of the sail. Only candidates that prove themselves in shadow get deployed.
Policy version
The turtle's decision rules as a signed, numbered release. Adjustments arrive checksummed, are acknowledged, and can always be rolled back one step.
Luffing
The flapping of a sail pointed into the wind. Our wind "sensor" is really just noticing where luffing starts.
Feathering
Turning the sail edge-on to the wind so it produces no force — the sailing equivalent of putting the car in neutral.
Fixed rudder
Our rudder does not move. It acts like the feathers on an arrow — a passive stabilizer — while the sail is the only thing we control.
Confidence score
A number attached to every estimate (wind, heading, position) saying how much we trust it. Low confidence means the turtle attempts less and re-measures more.
Deadband
A "close enough" zone around the target within which the servo does not move — small errors are cheaper to tolerate than to chase.
Sensor fusion / complementary filter
Blending two imperfect sensors so each covers the other's weakness — here, a drifty-but-smooth gyro with a noisy-but-honest compass.
Dead reckoning
Estimating your position from your last known fix plus your heading and speed since — what sailors did for centuries before GPS.
Tacking
Zig-zagging at angles to the wind to make progress toward an upwind destination.
Waypoint
A GPS coordinate on the mission route. The turtle sails to each in order.
Telemetry
The status messages each turtle sends home to hopeturtles.org: position, battery, machine state, and (proposed) nav internals with confidence scores.
