🐢 How a Hope Turtle Finds Its Way

The turtleOS navigation system — what our solar sailboats can do today, what we still have to prove, and the two-stream roadmap (turtle + shore) to full learning autonomy. Written for the whole team: no engineering background required.

July 2026 · Revised: design review + AOELL learning architecture · Only "Today" items are built

The Big IdeaA turtle senses, thinks, and trims — forever

Every Hope Turtle is a small autonomous sailboat. It has no motor for propulsion — the wind pushes it, and a tiny computer (the XIAO ESP32-S3, about the size of a postage stamp) decides everything. The turtle has exactly one moving part: the sail, rotated by a servo motor. The rudder is fixed. About three times every second, the computer runs one loop of the same routine:

How a sail-only boat actually steers — the honest version

This is the single most important correction from our design review. The turtle has no movable rudder. Trimming the sail does not point the boat the way a steering wheel points a car. It changes the force the wind puts on the boat; the fixed rudder, hull, and keel then convert that force into some mix of forward motion and turning:

Why this matters: our earlier plan assumed sail moves translate into predictable steering, and stacked automatic controllers on top of that assumption. The revised roadmap flips the order — first measure whether a 10° sail change produces a repeatable heading response, and only then automate. Until measured, an automatic controller wrapped around an unknown relationship is, as the review put it, "an elegant way to automate confusion."

Part OneWhat the turtle can do today Running now

The turtle's life story: five states

From the moment it powers on, the turtle is always in exactly one of five "states" — think of them as chapters of its voyage. The state is reported home to hopeturtles.org with every telemetry message, so we always know which chapter a turtle is in.

Why SAFE matters

A confused robot boat that keeps sailing is dangerous to itself. SAFE means: stop trying, "feather" the sail so the wind slides past, keep reporting position home. A human (or a recovery rule) decides what happens next.

What ARRIVAL honestly means

A sail-only boat with a fixed rudder can't park. ARRIVAL means: inside the target circle → feather the sail, report "arrived", and keep sending position while drifting. If drift carries it back out, it sails in again. No station-keeping is claimed.

The clever trick: finding the wind with no wind sensor

Sailboats need to know the wind direction, but wind vanes are fragile and our turtles don't carry one. Instead, the turtle does something a human sailor would recognize: it sweeps the sail slowly from one side to the other and feels for the flutter. When the sail points straight into the wind it stops "filling" and starts flapping — sailors call this luffing. A little angle sensor on the sail (the AS5600) feels that flapping as jitter.

The turtle re-runs this sweep every 10 minutes while sailing. The revised plan makes re-sweeps event-driven — sweep when confidence in the wind estimate drops, or after a big turn — with the fixed timer relaxing to a 30–60 minute backup.

What else is already working

🧭 Compass heading

A magnetometer (the GY-271 board) tells the turtle which way it's pointing, corrected for local magnetic declination.

📍 Waypoint bearing

The turtle carries a list of GPS waypoints, computes the great-circle bearing to the next one, and advances waypoints on arrival — progress is saved so a reboot doesn't lose it.

🎛️ Placeholder autopilot

A simple heading-error → sail-nudge formula exists in the code — but it's scaffolding, not a tuned controller. Until we measure how sail moves actually turn the boat (Part Three), no controller can honestly be tuned.

🛟 Basic safety net

GPS silent too long → SAFE. Battery low → feather the sail to save power. Every telemetry message reports the machine state to hopeturtles.org.

Quiet superpowers we already have. Three foundations make the experiments in Part Three possible without new infrastructure: GPS logging gives ground truth — position, course over ground, speed over ground, and progress toward or away from the waypoint; the AS5600 sail sensor reports where the sail actually is, not just where we told the servo to go; and the telemetry schema already carries values_json, confidence_json, and flags_json with GPS coordinates and separate device/server timestamps — every experiment logs straight through it.

Part TwoThe new hardware: our IMU Sourced & wired

The big July decision: every turtle gets an IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit) — a fingernail-sized motion sensor, the same kind of chip that lets your phone know when you rotate it. It gives the turtle heading, heel (tilt), turn rate, and a feel for wave disturbance — the senses everything in Part Three builds on.

A naming heads-up before anything else. The module we sourced is sold as an "MPU6050 module", but the supplier's spec sheet says the chip on the board is actually an MPU-9250 — a better chip. An MPU6050 senses rotation and tilt (6 measurements). The MPU-9250 adds a built-in compass, for 9 measurements in total — which is exactly what a sailboat wants. The catch: software written for one won't run on the other, so our driver must target the MPU-9250, and the first thing we do on the bench is ask the chip its identity (it answers with a code: 0x71 means MPU-9250). Cheap modules are sometimes mislabeled in both directions, so we check every batch.

What the IMU senses

🌀 Gyroscope

"How fast am I turning?" Silky-smooth from second to second, but slowly drifts over minutes — like a dancer counting spins with closed eyes.

📉 Accelerometer

"Which way is down?" Tells us how much the boat is heeled (tilted) — essential for correcting the compass on a leaning sailboat — and how roughly the waves are shaking it.

🧲 Magnetometer

"Which way is north?" Truthful on average but noisy moment-to-moment — and wrong when tilted, unless we correct it with the accelerometer.

🤝 Together: sensor fusion

Blend the gyro's smoothness with the compass's honesty and the accelerometer's sense of "down", and you get a heading that is smooth, true, and tilt-proof.

The address clash — and the one-jumper fix

All our sensors share a single two-wire party line called the I2C bus, and each device on it needs a unique address, like houses on a street. Problem: the IMU's factory address is 0x68 — the exact same number our clock chip (the DS3231) already uses. Two houses, one street number: the mail goes to the wrong place and the bus can't work.

The chip designers anticipated this: the module has a pin labeled AD0 (sometimes printed "ADO") that works like an address-select switch. Leave it low → 0x68. Connect it to 3.3 V → 0x69, which is free on our bus.

The question everyone asks: wire AD0 to the VCC pin on the IMU board, or to the XIAO's 3V3 pin? Either — they're the same wire! The IMU's VCC pin is fed from the XIAO's 3V3 pin, so both points carry the identical 3.3 V. Our standard: put a short jumper (or solder bridge) right on the IMU board, from AD0 to its own VCC pin. That way the fix travels with the module — any board strapped like this is plug-in-safe forever, no matter who wires it up later.

One hard rule: power the module from the XIAO's 3V3 pin, never from 5 V. The spec sheet's "3–5 V" tolerance applies to the VCC pin only. The AD0 pin connects straight to the sensor chip, which is a 3.3 V part — 5 V on AD0 can damage it. Powering everything at 3.3 V makes this a non-issue.

The full hookup

Bonus: the MPU-9250's built-in compass appears on the bus at its own address (0x0C), which doesn't clash with anything — including our current GY-271 compass at 0x0D. So during the changeover we can run both compasses side by side and compare them before deciding whether to retire the GY-271.

Part ThreeThe proposed nav system — measure first, automate second For review

With the IMU aboard, here is the plan. It is now an experimental ladder: each rung must be demonstrated on real water before the next is attempted, and every measured quantity carries a confidence score that decides what the turtle is allowed to do with it.

1 · A heading you can trust: sensor fusion

Today's compass-only heading is like a nervous witness — right on average, jittery in the moment, and unreliable when the boat heels. The fusion recipe (a "complementary filter") blends 98% gyro smoothness with 2% compass truth on every sample, while the accelerometer un-tilts the compass math:

The IMU also gives us spin detection: the gyro notices the boat starting to rotate long before the compass average does. Important honesty note — detection is all it gives us for now. We do not yet know whether any sail movement reliably arrests a spin, or accidentally worsens one. So spin events are flagged and reported home, and the "what should the sail do about it?" question is answered by experiment, not assumption.

2 · Sense fast, decide slowly, move rarely

The system's rhythm is deliberately layered. Sensors sample fast; decisions happen at boat speed; the servo — our biggest power drain — moves only when a change is worth making:

3 · The make-or-break experiments: does sail trim steer at all?

Before any autopilot is tuned, we run structured experiments and judge them against the GPS track:

🍃 Can we sense? (luff validation)

Repeated sweeps against a known reference wind, on real water and real chop. Output: a measured accuracy figure and a confidence score on every wind solve.

⛵ Can we go? (propulsion)

Hold a series of sail trims at each wind angle; log GPS speed and course per trim. Output: demonstrated trims that actually move the boat toward a goal.

🔄 Can we turn? (turning influence)

From steady sailing, step the sail ±10°, ±20° and record the heading response. Same input, same conditions — same result? The repeatability number is the go/no-go for any automatic steering.

🌪️ Can we stop a spin?

When the gyro flags a spin: does feathering help? Sheeting in? Nothing? Until measured, spin detection stays report-only.

Confidence scoring sits at the heart of all of this, not off in a diagnostics corner. The wind estimate, the heading, the position, and the sail→turn model each carry a confidence value, reported home in confidence_json. Low confidence narrows what the turtle may attempt: an uncertain wind solve blocks tack attempts; an uncertain steering model widens the deadband. The turtle acts boldly only on what it has proven.

4 · Sailing upwind: the tack sequence Much later

No sailboat can point straight into the wind — there's a roughly 90°-wide "no-go zone" where the sail just flaps. Sailors beat this by zig-zagging ("tacking"). A tack presumes everything above it on the ladder: a trusted wind solve, demonstrated propulsion, and repeatable turning authority. That is why it now sits deliberately near the end of the roadmap — attempting it earlier would be testing three unproven systems at once.

6 · A deeper safety net

🧮 Dead reckoning

If GPS drops out, the fused heading plus the last known speed keeps a short-term position estimate going — buying minutes of graceful sailing before the SAFE timer fires. Unlocked by the IMU.

🕵️ GPS sanity check

If GPS claims we're moving north but the compass insists we point south for minutes on end, something is lying (glitch, or spoofing). Sustained disagreement → SAFE.

🗺️ Geofence

A boundary drawn around the mission area. A turtle that drifts outside it goes SAFE and calls home, instead of wandering into a shipping lane.

💧 Hull watch

Moisture and temperature sensors inside the hull → SAFE plus an immediate distress telemetry packet the moment water shows up where it shouldn't.

🐕 Watchdog

A hardware timer that reboots the computer if the software ever hangs. Paired with mission persistence, a rebooted turtle resumes its voyage on its own.

📡 Nav telemetry

Heading error, wind solution + confidence, commanded and actual sail angle, and progress-made-good in every packet home — so we can replay and tune a turtle's decisions from shore.

Part FourLearning twice: AOELL and hopeturtles.org New dimension

The July revision added the piece the plan was missing. turtleOS is not a finished navigation controller — it is an instrumented sailing experiment that acts, observes the consequences, evaluates honestly, records enough context to explain itself, and learns. And it must learn twice: once aboard for the next decision, and again ashore — on hopeturtles.org — so the whole team, and eventually the whole fleet, improves.

The AOELL cycle: every action is a little experiment

We call the onboard loop AOELL: Act · Observe · Evaluate · Log · Learn. Even "do nothing" is a recorded action — hold periods are the control cases that let us tell a sail effect apart from a current or a wind shift that would have happened anyway. But the cycle isn't complete when the turtle updates itself. The records sail on to shore, where humans and (later) models interpret them and send back improved, versioned instructions:

📖 The log records reasoning

Every cycle answers seven questions: what did I do, what did I believe, why did I choose it, what did I predict, what did I observe, how did I score it, what did I update — plus which firmware, policy, and config were in force.

🤷 "Not evaluable" is not failure

A lost GPS fix or a wave-thrashed observation window must never become a false training label. The turtle is allowed — required — to say "I couldn't judge that one."

📦 Nothing is lost at sea

Connectivity is intermittent, so records queue aboard and upload in checksummed, acknowledged batches. A week-late upload appears in the week-old part of the voyage — never masquerading as live navigation.

🔒 No self-rewriting at sea

Onboard learning updates bounded state inside a versioned policy — logged and reversible. New policies arrive signed and checksummed, and the turtle keeps a one-step rollback.

Two development streams that must rejoin

This gives our work two parallel streams. Stream A is the turtleOS firmware — the sailing ladder from Part Three plus the AOELL recording machinery. Stream B is hopeturtles.org — the mission database, the batch ingestion, and above all the mission_review panel, the major later-phase destination everything aboard is working toward connecting to. Neither stream is useful alone: perfect logs nobody can interpret teach nothing, and a beautiful dashboard with no causal records explains nothing.

Inside mission_review: the shore-side half

The mission_review panel on hopeturtles.org is not a dashboard — it is a scientific notebook and review system. Its map draws four different things and refuses to blur them: where the turtle actually went, where the mission intended it to go, where turtleOS predicted an action would take it, and what was evaluated afterward. Keeping those distinct is how we tell three very different failures apart — wrong objective, wrong prediction, or a good decision defeated by the sea.

🗺️ Mission map + time scrubber

Actual / intended / predicted / evaluated layers, action markers, prediction error vectors, confidence shading. Drag the scrubber and every panel follows to the same moment and cycle.

📈 Synchronized timeline

Sail commanded vs. measured, wind estimate + confidence, heading vs. course over ground, VMG, battery, state changes — all on one aligned time axis, so causality can be seen instead of guessed.

🔍 Cycle inspector

Click any action: the turtle's belief before, the trigger, alternatives it rejected, its prediction, what actually happened at each horizon, and what it learned. Reviewers can confirm or question labels — originals are never erased.

📊 Sense-making views

Aggregations across many cycles: response by sail step size and wind angle, hold vs. intervention periods, energy per correction, prediction calibration ("70% confident" should be right ~70% of the time), per-turtle differences.

Machine learning grows in careful stages — response tables first (is there a learnable relationship at all?), then supervised models validated on held-out missions, then shadow mode where a candidate only says what it would do while the trusted policy keeps the sail. Only a model that beats simple baselines, stays calibrated, and knows when to say "I don't know" ever gets deployed — signed, versioned, monitored, and one step from rollback. No turtle silently rewrites its own brain at sea.

The revised governing principle: act deliberately, observe carefully, evaluate honestly, log completely — and learn twice: once aboard for the next decision, and again ashore so the whole team, and eventually the whole turtle fleet, can improve.

Part FiveThe experimental ladder — phases for review

We propose climbing in this order, with a measurable pass/fail gate at each rung. Rungs 2–5 are Stream A's sailing experiments; the Stream B build (mission_review) runs in parallel and meets them at the J milestones. Each phase earns the next. None of this starts until the team has reviewed this proposal.

  1. AOELL foundations. The cycle record (act, belief, reason, prediction, observation, evaluation, versions), the local queue, and resumable checksummed upload — so every experiment after this one explains itself. Gate: one cycle reconstructable from logs; delayed and duplicate uploads produce one ordered server record.
  2. Sensor fusion. Verify the IMU's identity, write the MPU-9250 driver, fuse gyro + compass + tilt into one trustworthy heading; spin detection as a reported event only. Gate: heading drift under 2°/min on the bench, correct at 30° of heel.
  3. Luff validation. Measure the wind sweep's real accuracy against a known reference, on water; attach a confidence score to every solve; log every sweep as an AOELL cycle. Gate: a measured accuracy figure — no more assumed numbers.
  4. Propulsion. Demonstrate that chosen sail trims produce real forward progress, judged by the GPS track, with hold periods as controls. Gate: repeatable progress-made-good at trial trims.
  5. Turning influence. Bounded ±5°/±10°/±20° sail steps against hold baselines; measure the course response at 30 s / 2 min / 5 min; quantify repeatability — including whether anything arrests a spin. Gate: a usable sail→turn model, or an honest "no" that reshapes the plan.
  6. Heading control. Only now, wrap a controller (PID or simpler) around the demonstrated model, with deadband and slew limits. Gate: holds a heading within ±10° in calm water.
  7. Tacking. The upwind zig-zag, plus a re-sweep after every turn. Gate: net upwind progress across a full tack sequence.
  8. Safety net. Dead reckoning, GPS sanity check, geofence, hull sensors, honest ARRIVAL. Gate: goes SAFE within 60 s of GPS loss; sail feathers within 1 s.
  9. Unattended endurance. Watchdog + automatic mission resume. Gate: a mid-mission reboot resumes the voyage with no human touch.

Meanwhile Stream B climbs its own ladder on hopeturtles.org — mission + AOELL database tables, idempotent batch ingestion, the mission_review replay (map, timeline, cycle inspector), annotation and comparison views, then staged models and the signed policy lifecycle. The two ladders meet at the J milestones: J1 first replayed voyage (after rung 1), J2 trustworthy training data (after the experiment rungs), J3 shadow mode at sea, and J4 the closed learning loop — the roadmap's definition of done for the learning architecture.

For the engineering-level task list behind these phases — file by file, checkbox by checkbox, both streams and the J milestones — see docs/navigation_roadmap.md in the repository. The full AOELL specification (event contract, database tables, API surface, ML stages, and statistical safeguards) is in docs/AOELL nav revision.md. AppendixLittle glossary

I2C bus
A two-wire "party line" that lets many sensors share the same pair of wires, each answering to its own address.
IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit)
A chip that senses rotation (gyroscope), tilt and motion (accelerometer), and — on our MPU-9250 — magnetic north (magnetometer).
AOELL
The onboard cycle — Act, Observe, Evaluate, Log, Learn. Every action, even "hold the sail," becomes a recorded experiment with a prediction and an honestly scored outcome.
mission_review
The planned analysis panel on hopeturtles.org: a synchronized map, timeline, and cycle inspector for replaying any voyage and understanding what the turtle believed, did, predicted, and observed.
Hold period
A stretch where the sail deliberately isn't moved. The control case — without holds, we can't tell a sail effect from a current or wind shift that would have happened anyway.
Shadow mode
A candidate model rides along recording what it would have done while the trusted policy keeps control of the sail. Only candidates that prove themselves in shadow get deployed.
Policy version
The turtle's decision rules as a signed, numbered release. Adjustments arrive checksummed, are acknowledged, and can always be rolled back one step.
Luffing
The flapping of a sail pointed into the wind. Our wind "sensor" is really just noticing where luffing starts.
Feathering
Turning the sail edge-on to the wind so it produces no force — the sailing equivalent of putting the car in neutral.
Fixed rudder
Our rudder does not move. It acts like the feathers on an arrow — a passive stabilizer — while the sail is the only thing we control.
Confidence score
A number attached to every estimate (wind, heading, position) saying how much we trust it. Low confidence means the turtle attempts less and re-measures more.
Deadband
A "close enough" zone around the target within which the servo does not move — small errors are cheaper to tolerate than to chase.
Sensor fusion / complementary filter
Blending two imperfect sensors so each covers the other's weakness — here, a drifty-but-smooth gyro with a noisy-but-honest compass.
Dead reckoning
Estimating your position from your last known fix plus your heading and speed since — what sailors did for centuries before GPS.
Tacking
Zig-zagging at angles to the wind to make progress toward an upwind destination.
Waypoint
A GPS coordinate on the mission route. The turtle sails to each in order.
Telemetry
The status messages each turtle sends home to hopeturtles.org: position, battery, machine state, and (proposed) nav internals with confidence scores.
